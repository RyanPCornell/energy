Gasoline Engine Thermal Efficiency
Typical gasoline engine: 20–35% thermal efficiency; best modern engines approach 40–45%U.S. DOE, EERE. Where the Energy Goes: Gasoline Vehicles. fueleconomy.gov ↗
~68–72% of fuel energy exits as exhaust heat and engine cooling lossesHeywood, J. B. (2018). Internal Combustion Engine Fundamentals (2nd ed.). McGraw-Hill.
Electric Motor & EV Efficiency
EV electric motor efficiency: 85–97%; combined battery-to-wheel efficiency ~77–85%U.S. DOE, EERE. Where the Energy Goes: Electric Vehicles. fueleconomy.gov ↗
EV well-to-wheel efficiency surpasses gasoline even after accounting for grid generation lossesSioshansi, R. & Denholm, P. (2009). Emissions impacts of plug-in hybrid vehicles. Environmental Science & Technology, 43(4), 1199–1204.
Drivetrain & Transmission Losses
Automatic transmissions: 86–94% efficient; manual: 94–97%; EV single-speed reducer: ~97–99%Murphey, P. C., et al. (2013). Energy Efficiency of Advanced Automatic Transmissions. SAE Int'l. doi:10.4271/2013-01-0993
Aerodynamic Drag & Rolling Resistance
Aerodynamic drag accounts for ~20% of tractive energy at highway speeds; lower in city drivingDavis, S. C., et al. (2022). Transportation Energy Data Book, Ed. 40. Oak Ridge National Laboratory. ORNL/TM-2021/2221.
Rolling resistance coefficient (Crr) for passenger tires: 0.007–0.015; contributes ~5–15% of tractive energySchuring, D. J. (1980). The Rolling Loss of Pneumatic Tires. Rubber Chemistry & Technology, 53(3), 600–727.
Regenerative Braking
Regen systems recover 60–80% of available braking energy under ideal conditionsGao, Y. & Ehsani, M. (2001). Electronic Braking System of EV and HEV. SAE Technical Paper 2001-01-2478.
Full hybrids (e.g., Toyota Prius) achieve 20–35% better fuel economy largely through regen braking + Atkinson-cycle engineHermance, D. & Sasaki, S. (1998). Hybrid Electric Vehicles Take to the Streets. IEEE Spectrum, 35(11), 48–52.
First Law of Thermodynamics
Energy is conserved — it cannot be created or destroyed, only converted between forms (ΔU = Q − W)Çengel, Y. A. & Boles, M. A. (2018). Thermodynamics: An Engineering Approach (9th ed.), Ch. 2. McGraw-Hill.
The low thermal efficiency of heat engines is a consequence of the Second Law (Carnot limit), not merely poor engineeringMoran, M. J., et al. (2018). Fundamentals of Engineering Thermodynamics (9th ed.). Wiley.
